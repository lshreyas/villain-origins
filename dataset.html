SECTION 01
SUMMARY

The dataset, at a glance

We tagged the primary antagonist in roughly five hundred Hollywood films, 1940 to early 2025, by country of origin. That sample is the entire substrate of every chart in the dossier. Below are the headline numbers.

A few things are worth being honest about up front. The sample is not a random draw; it's a curated set of films we judged well enough known to count. It skews to wide releases, Oscar nominees, and films that made it into the cultural memory; it under-represents direct-to-video, niche horror, and most non-English-language releases. The Hollywood industry made many tens of thousands of films across this period — the comparison below shows the gap.

SECTION 03
HOW WE BUILT IT

The process

There was no scrape and no API call. The dataset was assembled by hand, then iterated.

Initial pass

First, brainstorm: by decade, list the films you can name that have a clear primary antagonist. Bias toward films that won Oscars, did big box office, became franchises, or are routinely cited on best-of lists (AFI, BFI, Sight & Sound, IMDb top 250, Rotten Tomatoes critics' lists).

Origin tag

For each film, write down who the primary villain is and what country that person belongs to. For films where the villain is the Soviet Union, the German army, or "Al-Qaeda," the country tag is the obvious one. For films where the villain is a corporation, a cult, or a serial killer, the country tag is wherever the action is set (almost always the US).

Iteration

The first pass missed a lot. We added films when we noticed a decade was thin, when we remembered a franchise we'd skipped, or when a category looked off (e.g., the 1950s Red Scare was visibly under-represented after the first pass).

What got cleaned up

A second pass removed duplicates (films double-listed in two categories), films with the wrong year, films that aren't actually Hollywood (some Australian/UK productions), and a handful of TV miniseries that slipped in.

SECTION 04
WHAT COUNTS AS HOLLYWOOD

Inclusion criteria

"Hollywood" is fuzzy at the edges. Here's what we're using as the operational rule.

  • US-financed or US-distributed theatrical films. The major and mini-major studios, plus Sundance-style indies that got wide US distribution.
  • Wide release, mostly. If a film opened in fewer than ~50 theaters and made under $1M, it's probably not here even if it's good.
  • 1940 through early 2025. The 2020s decade is partial — through about 2024.
  • No TV, no streaming-originals (mostly). A few exceptions where the line is genuinely blurry: prestige Netflix/Apple films that played in theaters for an Oscar qualification.
  • International co-productions are in if the financing and distribution are mostly American (so e.g., the Bond films count; Crouching Tiger doesn't).
  • Documentaries are out. Animation is in.
SECTION 05
TAGGING ORIGIN

How we decide where the villain is from

Origin is the hardest call. The dataset uses eleven buckets: Germany, Russia/USSR, Middle East, North Korea, China, Japan, the UK, Latin America, USA (domestic), Vietnam, and Non-national.

The rule

Tag the primary antagonist's country — the one most viewers would associate with the threat. For Hans Gruber, that's Germany. For Drago, Russia. For Hannibal Lecter, the USA. For Sauron, Non-national.

Regional categories

Some categories collapse multiple countries. The Middle East category includes Iran, Iraq, Afghanistan, Saudi Arabia, Israel, Palestine, Libya, Syria, Pakistan, and a few generic-Arab fictional states. Latin America includes Mexico, Cuba, Colombia, El Salvador, and Brazil. The world map exhibit spreads regional counts across the actual countries; the other exhibits keep them grouped.

Non-national

Aliens, AI, monsters, ghosts, ancient demons, animals, and abstract evils (grief, ambition, time) all get tagged Non-national. Jaws, the Terminator, Pennywise, and Thanos all live here. So do, perhaps controversially, films where the villain is the human condition itself (e.g., Manchester by the Sea's grief).

Ambiguous cases

A few villains genuinely defy a single flag. We place them where most viewers locate them:

  • Keyser Söze — described as Hungarian-Turkish; we tag The Usual Suspects as USA because the crime is American and the film is read as a noir about American criminals.
  • Le Chiffre — a stateless banker; we tag Casino Royale as Non-national.
  • Hans Gruber — a German "terrorist for hire" in Die Hard. Germany.
  • Solomon Lane in the MI films — British MI6 alumnus running a Syndicate; we tag UK.
SECTION 06
OPEN QUESTIONS

Things we're not sure about

QUESTION 01
Should we drop films where the villain is American?

About 17% of our sample (88 of 500 films) have a domestic American villain. They dominate every chart after the 1970s. There's a legitimate argument for cutting them: the visualization is about Hollywood's view of the foreign Other, and including The Wolf of Wall Street alongside Rocky IV arguably mixes two different stories.

We kept them in. The biggest argument is that the same anxiety machinery produces both kinds of villain. The rise of the corporate raider on screen in the late 1980s is a story about American self-image just as much as Drago is a story about Soviets, and they coexisted in the same year. Dropping US villains would erase one of the most interesting findings: that America's loudest screen enemy has been America since the 1970s.

The compromise is the USA · DOMESTIC toggle above the streamgraph in Exhibit I. Switch it to REDACTED to see the shape of the chart without the American 17%; the foreign-villain story sits underneath.

QUESTION 02
How do you decide what's in the dataset at all?

Honest answer: there's no objective rule. The criteria above (wide release, Oscar / box office / cultural recognition, identifiable primary antagonist) are guidelines, not bright lines. The 1970s probably should include more horror; the 1990s probably has too many art-house dramas. We expanded categories that looked thin in earlier passes, which is a kind of bias: the more we noticed a gap, the more we filled it.

If we had infinite time, the more rigorous approach would be: pull every wide-release US film from a database (TMDB, IMDb), sample stratified by year and box office, tag the primary villain from synopses and Wikipedia, then weight by box office or recognition. We didn't do that; we did this. The dataset is most defensible as "films a reasonably well-read viewer can name."

QUESTION 03
Why is China underrepresented — bias or reality?

Reality, mostly. The dossier's Exhibit 5 walks through it: Hollywood needed China at the box office from the mid-2010s on, and studios that wanted import approval had every incentive to scrub Chinese villains. The dataset is faithful to what the screen actually showed.

But there's a sample concern too. We may have missed some lower-profile films with Chinese villains that we don't remember. If you have candidates, the right place to add them is the repo.

SECTION 07
WHAT'S MISSING

Things we'd add

A few enrichments would make this dataset substantially better. None are in yet.

  • IMDb / Rotten Tomatoes scores. Would let us weight by quality, distinguish forgotten clunkers from canon, and check whether bad villain choices correlate with bad films.
  • Box office, per film. Same idea: weight by cultural footprint. Rocky IV and a barely-released 80s thriller should not count equally.
  • Secondary antagonists. We only track the primary. The Avengers tagged as Non-national (Loki) misses the entire Hydra plotline.
  • Cast nationality and political context. Whether a villain is played by an actual Russian actor vs. a stage Russian accent is a different signal.
  • TV and streaming series. The villain shape of Homeland, The Americans, Killing Eve, 24 would be very informative.
  • Synopsis-level NLP. Pull every Wikipedia plot summary in this era, identify the antagonist with a language model, tag origin automatically, then human-check. We didn't.
